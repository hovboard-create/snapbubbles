History of Bubble Wrap: Invented as Wallpaper in 1957

Bubble wrap was an accident. Two engineers in a Hawthorne, New Jersey garage tried to invent textured wallpaper, made something nobody wanted to put on their walls, and stumbled their way into one of the most successful packaging products of the 20th century. The story takes about three pivots.

1957: The wallpaper experiment

The inventors were Alfred Fielding, an engineer, and Marc Chavannes, a Swiss inventor who had immigrated to the United States. They were trying to design a "plastic wallpaper" — sheets with a 3D textured surface that could be applied to interior walls for a modern, mid-century aesthetic.

Their process was straightforward: take two sheets of plastic, seal them together in a way that traps pockets of air between them, and you've got a textured surface. The prototype worked. The wallpaper, however, did not. Customers couldn't get past the look — air pockets behind plastic film read as "industrial," not "interior design." It was a commercial flop.

Pivot one: greenhouse insulation

Fielding and Chavannes didn't give up. The trapped air pockets were unusually good thermal insulators, so they reframed the product for greenhouses. A double layer of plastic with insulating air pockets could keep heat in during winter nights and reduce heating costs for commercial growers.

This pitch was more sensible than wallpaper, but greenhouse operators preferred existing solutions. The greenhouse market never materialized. Two failed product categories in, the company they had founded — Sealed Air Corporation, incorporated in 1960 — was running out of runway.

Pivot two: the IBM moment

The breakthrough came in 1959 when a marketer named Frederick W. Bowers, working with Sealed Air, made a pitch to IBM. IBM had just launched the 1401 — a new computer that needed to be shipped intact across the country. Computers in the late 1950s were fragile, expensive, and routinely arrived damaged. IBM was looking for a better protective packaging material.

Bowers demonstrated that the plastic-wallpaper-greenhouse-insulation product was actually an excellent shock absorber. The trapped air pockets compressed and rebounded, distributing impact across a wide surface. IBM signed on. The 1401 shipped in bubble wrap, arrived intact, and the rest of the packaging industry took notice.

The product Sealed Air had been trying to sell for years finally found its market — by being completely unrelated to its original purpose.

Becoming a cultural object

Bubble wrap became ubiquitous as a packaging material throughout the 1960s and 70s. But the cultural shift — the moment it stopped being purely industrial and became something people *enjoyed* — happened more gradually. Office workers receiving shipments noticed they couldn't resist popping the leftover material. Kids fought over it. Recipients of fragile packages saved sheets specifically for popping later.

By the 1990s, the appeal had become enough of a cultural phenomenon that Sealed Air began acknowledging it explicitly. In 2001, the company sponsored the first Bubble Wrap Appreciation Day, held on the last Monday in January. It's still observed annually.

The "iBubble Wrap" detour

In 2015, Sealed Air introduced "iBubble Wrap" — a redesigned version where the bubbles were connected, allowing them to be inflated on demand at the packaging facility. The new design used less plastic and stored more efficiently, but it also couldn't be popped. Outrage was real and immediate. The company added a "poppable" variant to the product line within a couple years. The lesson, in product terms: when a side effect becomes the main attraction, you can't just remove it.

The Flash era and digital revival

Virtual bubble wrap appeared on the web almost as soon as Adobe Flash made interactive web content easy. Early-2000s sites — including the original snapbubbles.com — let people pop bubbles in a browser instead of waiting for a fragile package to arrive. Most of those sites went dark when Flash was discontinued in December 2020.

Modern HTML5 makes the original idea work again, this time on phones, with procedural sound and no plugin required. The current Snap Bubbles is a rebuild in that lineage — same core idea, modernized for a post-Flash internet, with three modes (Zen, Speed, Survival) instead of the original endless sheet.

Why the origin story matters

The history of bubble wrap is one of the cleanest examples in product design of a side effect being more valuable than the intended product. It started life as wallpaper. It became insulation. It accidentally became packaging. And then — without any deliberate effort from the inventors — it became one of the most recognizable stress-relief objects in the world. Each pivot was driven by the customer, not the original spec.

If you'd like to participate in roughly 70 years of accidental product success, there are bubbles waiting for you here.
